## Overview

### What actually makes a startup grow so fast?

Some startups spend years trying to find product-market fit. Others seem to go from an idea to millions in revenue almost overnight.

For my final thesis at the University of Ljubljana, I wanted to understand what happens behind that growth.

I chose to study **Lovable**, a Swedish AI startup that lets people build applications using natural language.

Its growth was remarkable. In July 2025, Lovable reported reaching $100 million in annual recurring revenue (ARR), just eight months after reaching its first $1 million in ARR.

But I was more interested in what happened before those numbers.

**Was Lovable successful because of its product, its founders, market timing, or the way it built in public?**

I explored the company's development through public interviews, company announcements, investor publications, and academic research.

What I found changed how I think about building and growing startups.

## The starting point

### Lovable didn't start as Lovable

In June 2023, co-founder Anton Osika shared an open-source side project called GPT Engineer.

The idea was simple: describe what you want to build in natural language and let AI generate the code.

The project attracted significant attention from developers and eventually became the foundation for a commercial product.

But turning that attention into a business wasn't straightforward.

![Product evolution from GPT Engineer (2023) to GPT Engineer App (early 2024) to Lovable (November 2024)](/media/lovable-evolution.png)

The team launched early commercial versions of GPT Engineer App in 2024. Despite initial interest, they struggled to get people to use the product consistently.

People were interested in generating code, but what many actually needed was a way to build complete, working applications.

This distinction became one of the most important discoveries in my research.

## Finding 01 — Product-market fit

### Attention doesn't mean people want your product

One of the most interesting things about Lovable's story is that its early launches attracted attention without achieving the desired user retention.

This challenged an assumption I had about startup growth.

It's easy to see a successful launch on Product Hunt, thousands of GitHub stars, or a viral post and assume a product is doing well.

But attention tells us that people are curious. It doesn't tell us whether they will keep using the product.

![Attention signals (GitHub stars, Product Hunt rankings, social engagement) compared with product value signals (activation, repeat usage, retention, paying customers)](/media/attention-vs-value.png)

For Lovable, the early launches became opportunities to learn.

The team moved beyond generating code and focused on helping users build complete applications. That meant adding capabilities such as databases, authentication, and deployment, including an integration with Supabase.

The product evolved from an interesting technical experiment into a more useful solution for founders, independent builders, and agencies.

> **The lesson:** A successful launch gets people to try your product. A useful product gives them a reason to return.

#### How can you apply this?

Before investing more time or money in growth, ask yourself:

- Are users coming back after their first experience?
- What are they actually trying to accomplish?
- Where do they stop using the product?
- Would they use it again without being reminded?
- Are they willing to pay for the value it creates?

These questions are more useful than simply tracking views, likes, or registrations.

A smaller group of returning users can teach you more about product value than a large launch audience that never comes back.

## Finding 02 — Building in public

### What if marketing and product development were part of the same process?

The second part of my thesis focused on _building in public_.

Before researching Lovable, I mainly associated building in public with founders sharing their progress on social media.

But the research revealed a more useful way to think about it.

**Building in public isn't just about showing people what you're building. It's about involving them in the process of building it.**

Lovable's journey illustrates this in several ways.

The public release of GPT Engineer allowed developers to experiment with the technology and provide feedback. Later, early users could try commercial versions and expose problems that the team hadn't fully anticipated.

As the product evolved, users also began publicly sharing applications they had created.

![The original GPT Engineer announcement on X, the GPT Engineer GitHub repository, and a publicly shared Lovable project](/media/build-in-public-collage.png)

This produced two forms of value.

First, the company could learn from actual user behavior. Second, those projects showed potential users what was possible with the product.

Instead of separating product development, community building, and distribution, these activities could reinforce one another.

#### The build-in-public feedback loop

![The build-in-public feedback loop: share what you're building, let people try it, observe behaviour and collect feedback, improve the product, share what users create](/media/feedback-loop.png)

This is the practical framework I took away from the case.

Public content attracts interest. Direct product access turns that interest into real usage. Feedback helps improve the product. Better user outcomes create new examples worth sharing.

But the loop only works when people can experience the product's value.

Lovable's co-founder Fabian Hedin later reflected that keeping the product behind a closed waitlist for too long had delayed valuable feedback.

This was another important finding for me: you can communicate openly while still making it difficult for people to help you improve.

#### How can you apply this?

If you're building a product, you don't need a large following to start building in public.

You can begin by sharing a problem you're exploring, a prototype, a feature you're testing, or something surprising you learned from users.

The important part is to make each public update useful to your product development.

Before posting, ask: **What am I trying to learn from this?**

Then give people a way to try the product and act on what you discover.

You also don't need to share everything. Customer information, sensitive technology, and strategic decisions can remain private.

The goal is useful openness, not complete transparency.

## Finding 03 — Growth factors

### A great product alone isn't enough

Another question I explored was which factors contributed to Lovable's rapid growth.

I grouped them into four categories: team, strategy, market, and capital.

![Four growth factors: team and strategy as internal factors, market and capital as external factors](/media/growth-factors.png)

#### Team — Experience creates an advantage

Lovable's founders, Anton Osika and Fabian Hedin, had previously worked together at Depict.ai.

They brought experience in AI, software development, building products, and operating startups.

They also had existing connections within the startup and investment ecosystem.

This suggests that an early-stage team's advantage isn't just technical ability. It's also the ability to make decisions, work together, and access knowledge and resources.

#### Strategy — Adapt before you scale

Lovable didn't simply grow its original product.

The team learned from unsuccessful launches, changed its positioning, and expanded what users could accomplish.

One of the important strategic changes was moving from a tool focused on code generation to a platform for building complete applications.

> Sometimes the most important growth decision is what you change before trying to grow.

#### Market — Timing matters

Lovable entered a market where generative AI capabilities were improving rapidly and the cost of using language models was falling.

At the same time, more people were becoming interested in using AI to create software.

These conditions created an opportunity that would have been much harder to pursue a few years earlier.

But Lovable's first unsuccessful launches also show that being in a growing market doesn't guarantee success.

A favorable market creates an opportunity. The product still needs to meet a real need.

#### Capital — Resources can accelerate what already works

Venture capital gave Lovable resources to hire, improve infrastructure, develop the product, and expand operations.

But funding is not proof of product-market fit or long-term success.

Capital can accelerate growth, but it can also increase costs and expectations.

The practical lesson I took away is to distinguish between finding something that works and having the resources to make it bigger.

![Lovable's publicly reported ARR from launch in November 2024 to $400M in March 2026, with funding rounds marked below](/media/growth-timeline.png)

What stands out isn't just the speed of growth, but how product development, market conditions, and access to capital interacted over time.

## Finding 04 — Distribution

### What if your users became your best marketing channel?

One of the most useful connections I found was between building in public and _product-led growth_.

Product-led growth means that the product itself plays a central role in attracting, activating, and retaining users.

Lovable's self-service model allowed people to experience its value before committing to a paid subscription.

Users could build something, see the result, and decide whether the product was useful.

Some also shared their creations publicly.

![Describe an idea, build a working application, publish and share the result](/media/describe-build-share.png)

This gave Lovable a useful combination: its own public communication created awareness, while users' projects demonstrated what the product could do.

A potential customer didn't have to rely only on a company's promise. They could see examples of actual output.

> **The insight:** People can discover a product through content, but the product must deliver the value that makes them stay.

#### How can you apply this?

Think about whether your product naturally creates something users would want to share.

It could be a website, a design, a report, a video, or a measurable result.

If users already have a reason to share their outcomes, you may be able to make those outcomes easier to publish and discover.

This can help new users understand the product without relying entirely on conventional advertising.

However, sharing must remain useful to users. A distribution feature that doesn't benefit them can quickly become unwanted promotion.

## Putting it into practice

### How would I use these lessons to launch a new product?

Based on the research, I would approach an early-stage product through five steps.

![A five-step loop: define a hypothesis, show an early prototype, make the product accessible, measure real usage, improve and share what you learned](/media/five-step-framework.png)

#### 01 / Start with a clear assumption

Write down who the product is for, what problem it solves, and what outcome users should achieve.

Avoid treating these assumptions as facts.

#### 02 / Show something early

Share a working prototype or early product with relevant users.

Explain what you're testing and make it easy for them to respond.

#### 03 / Make the product accessible

Don't rely entirely on waitlists, announcements, or demonstrations.

Where possible, let users experience the core value themselves.

#### 04 / Measure behavior, not just interest

Look at activation, repeat use, retention, and willingness to pay.

Use this information alongside interviews and feedback to understand where the product falls short.

#### 05 / Turn learning into product improvements

Share meaningful updates, improve the product, and show what real users are achieving with it.

Repeat the process.

The aim isn't to go viral. It's to create a system where every launch, experiment, and user interaction makes the product better.

## Reflection

### Building in public isn't a growth hack

When I started researching Lovable, I wanted to understand how a startup could grow so quickly and whether building in public had played a major role.

What I learned was more nuanced.

Lovable's growth was associated with a combination of experienced founders, strategic product changes, favorable market conditions, access to venture capital, and public engagement with users.

Building in public was valuable because it helped connect several of these activities. It created opportunities to learn from users, develop a community, and attract more people to the product.

But it wasn't a standalone explanation for the company's growth.

My research was based on one case and publicly available information, so it can't establish exactly how much each factor contributed. The financial figures also came mainly from company announcements and media reports rather than independently audited data.

Still, the case left me with three ideas I want to carry into future projects.

**Build for actual use, not just attention.**

**Treat distribution as something you can design into your product.**

**Use public feedback to learn faster, not simply to build an audience.**

The biggest shift in my thinking is that product development and marketing don't always need to be separate activities.

When done intentionally, building in public can help connect them.

And for an early-stage founder with limited resources, that connection can be valuable.
